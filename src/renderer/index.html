Board-Level Repair & Micro-Soldering

          
Researched guides, symptom playbooks, and built-in boardview + PDF viewers with a reference library for your own schematics and datasheets.

        

      

      

        
        
        
        
        
        
        
        
      


      
      

        

          
Is this for you?

          
Board-level repair means fixing the motherboard itself — components, traces, BGAs — not swapping parts. It needs a proper bench, a steady hand under a microscope, and a methodical approach. Start on scrap boards, learn to read schematics + boardviews, and build up slowly.

        

        

          
iFixit repair guides

          
Step-by-step teardowns and repair guides for almost any device. Type a model to jump straight to its guides.

          

            
            
            
          

        

        

          
The bench — what to buy, in order

          
Buy first (can’t work without):

          
    
            
  • Temperature-controlled soldering station — JBC or Hakko are the pro standards; fast heat-up and stable temp matter more than wattage. A fine conical/bent tip for micro work.
    • 
            
  • Stereo microscope — not a luxury for board work; you’re soldering parts too small to see reliably by eye (down to 01005). 7×–45× zoom, good ring light.
    • 
            
  • Hot-air rework station — for connectors, shields and BGAs. Airflow and temp both need care or you lift pads/warp the board.
    • 
            
  • Multimeter with diode mode — your primary diagnostic; diode-mode and resistance readings tell you where a rail is shorted.
    • 
            
  • Consumables — good flux (paste + pen), leaded 63/37 solder ~0.3 mm (easier than lead-free), ChipQuik low-temp removal alloy, solder wick, 99% IPA, lint-free wipes.
    • 
            
  • Fixturing — a PCB holder/fixture, fine curved + straight tweezers.
    • 
          

          
Add next: a DC bench power supply (current-limited — for voltage injection and watching current draw), a preheater (even bottom-heat so big chips come off without stress).

          
Advanced: thermal camera (instantly shows the hot shorted component), BGA reball stencils + kit, ultrasonic cleaner.

        

        

          
Core techniques

          
    
            
  • Flux is everything — more than you think. It cleans, wets and carries heat. Clean residue with IPA afterwards.
    • 
            
  • Heat control — leaded solder flows ~315–345 °C on the iron; hot air typically ~350–400 °C with moderate airflow for connectors, shielding adjacent plastics with Kapton.
    • 
            
  • Removing a chip/connector — pre-heat the board, flood with flux, even hot-air circles, lift only when solder is fully molten (never pry cold — that’s how pads tear).
    • 
            
  • Low-temp removal — ChipQuik lowers the melting point so you can lift delicate parts with far less heat.
    • 
            
  • Pad/trace repair — scrape to clean copper, tin, and jumper with fine enamelled wire; re-establish the net from the boardview.
    • 
            
  • BGA reball — remove, wick the pads clean, flux, stencil + balls, reflow, align, reflow onto the board. Practice this a lot on scrap.
    • 
          

        

        

          
Safety & good habits

          
    
            
  • ESD — wrist strap + mat; logic boards are static-sensitive.
    • 
            
  • Leaded solder hygiene — fume extraction, wash hands, don’t eat at the bench.
    • 
            
  • Disconnect the battery before rework; discharge, and never hot-air over a connected Li-ion cell.
    • 
            
  • Practice on dead boards, and keep the original dump/data before you change anything.
    • 
          

        

      


      
      

        

          
Why this matters

          
Once you understand the handful of circuits a board is built from, a meter reading stops being a number and starts telling a story. These are the building blocks you meet on every laptop, phone and console board.

        


        

          
Reading a power rail (the foundation)

          
A "rail" is a voltage the board generates (e.g. 3.3 V, 1.8 V, VCORE). Every rail has a web of decoupling capacitors to ground, so in diode mode / resistance to ground a healthy rail reads a small-to-moderate value that drifts upward as those caps charge from your meter. A hard ~0 Ω / 0.000 V that doesn't move = something on that rail is shorted to ground (usually a cracked MLCC or a dead FET). That single idea drives most no-power diagnosis: measure the rail, decide "short or not", then hunt the part.

        


        

          
Buck converter (step-down) — the workhorse

          
Turns a higher rail into a lower one efficiently. A PWM controller switches a high-side FET and low-side FET in turn, feeding an inductor (coil) and output caps; a feedback line tells the controller to hold the target voltage. Most of a board's rails are bucks.

          
    
            
  • Shorted low-side FET → output rail dead-shorts to ground (classic "rail short").
    • 
            
  • Open inductor → no output (often blows after a FET fails).
    • 
            
  • Bad feedback / caps → wrong or unstable voltage.
    • 
            
  • So: rail shorted → suspect the low FET/output caps; rail missing but input + enable present → suspect coil/controller/feedback.
    • 
          

        


        

          
LDO (linear regulator)

          
A simpler, less efficient step-down for small/quiet rails. Three things matter: input, enable, output. Input + enable present but no output = dead LDO (or its output is shorted downstream and it's folding back). Great news for diagnosis: easy to reason about.

        


        

          
Boost converter (step-up)

          
Steps voltage up — the backlight rail (~40 V from ~12 V) is a boost. Inductor + switch FET + diode + feedback. If enable/PWM is missing or the feedback is broken, it won't boost — which is exactly the backlight no-boost case.

        


        

          
Power sequencing & PWRGOOD

          
Rails must come up in order: the EC enables rail A, waits for its PWRGOOD, then enables rail B, and so on up to the CPU/SoC. If any rail fails to assert PWRGOOD, the whole chain stalls — so "no power" is often one early rail that's shorted or missing, not the CPU. Find the first rail that doesn't come up and work from there.

        


        

          
USB-C Power Delivery (PD)

          
The port's CC1/CC2 lines carry a negotiation. Pull-up/pull-down resistances advertise roles; the PD controller then requests 5 / 9 / 15 / 20 V and the source switches VBUS up. That's why a dead CC line (blown ESD diode / cracked trace) leaves a port that only works one way round, and why a stuck PD controller sits at 5 V and never boosts to 20 V.

        


        

          
Battery charger IC & FETs

          
The charger IC regulates current/voltage into the pack, sensing current through tiny current-sense resistors and switching through the battery FETs. The fuel gauge talks to the system over SMBus. Knowing this, "won't charge" vs "no battery detected" point to different parts of the same chain (sense R / charger enable vs battery FET / gauge / SMBus).

        


        

          
Clocks, resets & the EC

          
Chips need a clock (crystal/oscillator) and the right reset release to run. A dead EC crystal = no power sequence at all. Resets are usually released once rails are good — a stuck-low reset holds a chip off. These don't show on a meter; a scope or a known-good swap confirms them.

        


        

          
Decoupling capacitors — why they short rails

          
Every rail is peppered with MLCC decoupling caps to ground. They fail short (mechanical cracks from board flex or heat), which instantly drags that rail to ground — the single most common "mystery short". That's why short-finding (injection + freeze/thermal) so often ends at one tiny cracked cap.

        

      


      
      

        

          
Before you test anything

          
    
            
  • Power off and discharge the board. In-circuit readings are only a rough screen — parallel caps/resistors/diodes on the same net can make a good part look bad and vice-versa.
    • 
            
  • When a reading looks wrong, confirm against the schematic/boardview, and where you can, lift one end or remove the part to test it properly.
    • 
            
  • Use diode mode for junctions and resistance (ohms) for passives; a reading of 0.000 V / 0 Ω usually means a dead short, OL means open.
    • 
          

        


        

          
MOSFETs

          
Types you'll meet: N-channel (most common, low-side switches & the low FET in buck converters), P-channel (high-side load switches / reverse-protection), logic-level (turn on from ~1.8–3.3 V gate), dual-N in one package, and synchronous buck pairs (high + low FET feeding a coil). Always get the pinout from the datasheet — G/D/S placement varies.

          
Body-diode test (N-channel), part powered off:

          
    
            
  • Diode mode. First short all three legs briefly to discharge the gate.
    • 
            
  • Black on Drain, Red on Source → body diode forward, reads ~0.4–0.6 V. Reverse the probes → OL. (P-channel: polarity reversed — body diode points the other way.)
    • 
            
  • Gate-charge check: touch Red to Gate for a moment to charge it, then Drain↔Source should now read near 0 both ways (channel turned on). Shorting the gate to source again turns it back off. High-threshold parts may not fully turn on from a meter.
    • 
            
  • Bad: ~0 V both ways from the start = shorted (the usual failure — often D–S short that drags a rail down). Never turns on after gate charge, or gate leaks to source = dead.
    • 
          

          
A normal body-diode reading only proves one junction behaves at low current — it is NOT an RDS(on) or switching test. Treat in-circuit results as a screen.

        


        

          
Diodes, Schottky & TVS

          
    
            
  • Diode mode: forward ~0.15–0.3 V (Schottky) or ~0.4–0.7 V (silicon); reverse OL.
    • 
            
  • 0 V both ways = shorted (common on TVS/ESD diodes that took a surge — they short to protect, and then hold a rail down). OL both ways = open.
    • 
          

        


        

          
Capacitors

          
    
            
  • Ceramic (MLCC): the classic rail-short culprit. In diode/ohms a good decoupling cap reads high/OL (big banks read a short rise as they charge). A hard 0 Ω short on a cap bank = one cracked MLCC — find it with injection + freeze/thermal. You can't read value in-circuit; look for cracks/burn marks.
    • 
            
  • Electrolytic / polymer: check for bulging/leaking; measure ESR (an ESR meter in-circuit is gold) — high ESR = tired cap even if value is OK.
    • 
          

        


        

          
Resistors, current-sense, inductors, coils, fuses

          
    
            
  • Resistor: measure ohms (best out-of-circuit). Open = burnt. Current-sense resistors are milliohms (often marked R010 = 0.010 Ω) — read ~0 Ω; open one kills a rail's feedback.
    • 
            
  • Inductor / coil / ferrite bead: should read ~0–few Ω. Open = blown (common in buck outputs after a FET fails). A shorted-turns fault won't show on a meter — needs scope/injection.
    • 
            
  • Fuse / zero-ohm link: 0 Ω = good, OL = blown. Tiny fuses sit right behind charge ports & connectors — check these early on "no power/no charge".
    • 
            
  • Crystal/oscillator: a meter won't test it — scope the output or swap a known-good.
    • 
          

        

        
Method/readings informed by electricalflux, Bettlink and Industrial Monitor Direct. Exact numbers vary by part — check the datasheet.

      


      
      

        

          
Read this first

          
    
            
  • These are starting points, not gospel — every board differs. Big boards and large ground/copper planes sink heat, so they need more airflow/temp or a preheater. Start low, add heat, keep the nozzle moving.
    • 
            
  • Leaded 63/37 melts ~183 °C, flows nicely with an iron at 320–345 °C. Lead-free (SAC) melts ~217 °C, iron 350–380 °C, and hot air hotter still. Add a little leaded solder to a lead-free joint to lower its melting point when reworking.
    • 
            
  • Protect neighbours with Kapton tape; preheat to cut thermal shock (stops pad-lift and cracked MLCCs). "Overheating the neighbourhood turns a port job into a chip job."

Soldering iron & tips — your Aixun

          
    
            
  • Temps: leaded 320–345 °C, lead-free 350–380 °C. If a joint isn't flowing, it's usually too little tip mass or no flux — not just low temperature. Go hotter briefly rather than dwelling long.
    • 
            
  • Micro / bent tip (fine I or small bent): 0201/0402 passives, QFN corners, fine enamelled-wire jumpers, lifted-pad repair, IC legs.
    • 
            
  • Chisel / knife tip (bigger): connectors, ground pins, big pads, through-hole, and drag-soldering IC rows — the mass holds heat so you're done faster with less total damage.
    • 
            
  • Conical / bent medium: general-purpose SMD and point work.
    • 
            
  • Rule of thumb: use the biggest tip that fits — more thermal mass means a faster joint and less dwell time on the board.
    • 
          

        

      


      
      

        

          
HDMI port — PlayStation (PS4 / PS5)

          
    
            
  1. Strip the board out, clean, and inspect the old port + pads under the scope. Note the orientation and the anchor legs.
    2. 
            
  2. Preheat the board (~200 °C) — consoles have huge ground planes; cold boards lift pads.
    3. 
            
  3. Flood with flux. Hot air ~400–450 °C, high airflow (~100–120), nozzle often removed to spread heat over the whole port. Kapton-shield the nearby retimer/encoder chip — that's the expensive thing to cook.
    4. 
            
  4. Lift only when every pin is molten — never pry a cold port or you'll tear pads. Clean pads with wick + flux, inspect.
    5. 
            
  5. Tin, place the new port, anchor the 4 mechanical legs first for alignment/strength, then reflow the signal pins. Inspect every joint under the scope; test for shorts before powering.
    6. 
          

          
Technique/temps from iFixit, RetroSix and kasynparts. Start lower and work up on your board.

        

        

          
Replacing a shorted ceramic cap (MLCC) on a rail

          
    
            
  1. Find the shorted cap first (resistance → voltage injection → freeze spray/thermal to spot the hot one).
    2. 
            
  2. Remove it: hot air ~300–330 °C, low airflow, with tweezers — or add leaded/ChipQuik and roll it off with the iron. Don't launch the part into a neighbour.
    3. 
            
  3. Wick the pads clean, flux, place a same value & voltage cap (match the footprint). If you don't know the value, read it from the schematic or an identical point.
    4. 
            
  4. Reflow, clean, and re-measure the rail isn't still shorted before powering.
    5. 
          

        

        

          
Lifted pad / broken trace

          
    
            
  1. Scrape back to clean copper on the trace, tin it.
    2. 
            
  2. Jumper with fine enamelled wire to the next via/component on that net (get the net from the boardview).
    3. 
            
  3. Tack both ends, verify continuity, then protect with UV solder-mask or a dab of epoxy so it can't flex and break again.
    4. 
          

        

        

          
USB-C charge port & Power Delivery (no charge)

          
    
            
  1. Rule out the easy stuff first: try a known-good PD charger + cable, try the other USB-C port (some don't support charging), and do an EC drain (hold power 30 s, no charger/battery).
    2. 
            
  2. CC lines are the handshake. The PD controller reads a resistance on CC1/CC2 (whichever matches plug orientation) to detect the charger. Idle, a healthy CC pin sits around ~1.7 V. Both CC at 0 V with no 5 V = PD-controller fault or a short. Charges in one orientation only = a blown ESD diode or fractured trace on that CC line.
    3. 
            
  3. Check the CC ESD/TVS diodes near the port in diode mode (a shorted one kills that CC). Check the port pins aren't bent/dirty — negotiation is physically impossible if they are.
    4. 
            
  4. Confirm VBUS (5 V in) reaches the board and the PD controller's own rail is present. Stuck at 5 V and never boosting to 20 V = PD controller, not the port (e.g. TI TPS6598x / Cypress CCG — get the part + schematic).
    5. 
            
  5. Repair: reflow or replace the port (hot air ~350–400 °C, big nozzle, preheat, Kapton neighbours), replace shorted CC ESD diodes, then re-test negotiation.
    6. 
          

          
CC behaviour/readings from Microsoft Q&A and nepguru. Confirm part numbers on the schematic.

        


        

          
Battery charging path & FETs (won't charge / "no battery detected")

          
Path: adapter → adapter-detect → input FETs → charger IC → current-sense resistors → battery FETs → battery & fuel gauge. Work along it:

          
    
            
  1. Adapter detect (ACDET/ADAPDET) — if the board doesn't "see" the adapter it won't charge. A bad DC jack/port can read 0 V here.
    2. 
            
  2. Input rail reaches the charger IC? Check the charger IC's supply and enable.
    3. 
            
  3. Current-sense resistors around the charger IC (milliohm, R010-type) — an open one kills the feedback and charging stops.
    4. 
            
  4. Battery FETs (often a dual-N pack between charger and battery): a shorted or open battery FET = no charge, or the system never detects a battery. Test as MOSFETs.
    5. 
            
  5. Fuel gauge + SMBus (SMClk/SMData) — "no battery detected" with good FETs points here, or at a dead gauge / pack.
    6. 
          

        


        

          
Backlight circuit (image present, screen dark)

          
    
            
  1. Confirm it's the backlight — shine a torch at the panel; if you can faintly see the desktop, the image/GPU is fine and it's the backlight. An external monitor working also isolates it to panel/cable/backlight.
    2. 
            
  2. Backlight fuse — check continuity (power off). Powered, ~12.6 V on both sides means the supply reaches the boost stage but it isn't boosting. A blown fuse means a downstream short — find it before fitting the same-rating fuse or it'll just blow again.
    3. 
            
  3. Boost output — healthy is ~39–49 V. Seeing ~8.5 V or ~12 V = not boosting; near 0 V = a short to ground on that line (or a blown via/trace).
    4. 
            
  4. Enable & PWM — the driver needs BKL_EN ≥ ~2.7 V and a BKL_PWM signal; missing either (from the EC/GPU) means no boost. A broken feedback trace to the LED-driver IC is a classic failure — jumpering it can confirm the diagnosis.
    5. 
            
  5. Panel side — bad LCD connector/cable (a backlight cable in upside-down is the single most common one), or a dead LED string in the panel.
    6. 
          

          
Voltages/method from iFixit & Rossmann-forum MacBook threads and this guide — MacBook-specific numbers; always check the model's schematic. LED-driver work is usually BGA hot-air rework.

        


        

          
Won't power on — the EC / power-sequence path

          
    
            
  1. Confirm the always-on rail (e.g. 3.3 V ALW / RTC / "G3" rails) is present — the EC runs from it even when "off".
    2. 
            
  2. Press power and watch for the sequence: power button → EC → enable the primary rails → PWRGOOD → CPU/SoC. If it dies instantly, go to the short-to-ground playbook (Fault-Finding).
    3. 
            
  3. Check the RTC/CMOS battery and the EC's clock/crystal — a dead EC clock = no power sequence.
    4. 
            
  4. No single rail coming up: find the regulator/enable for that rail on the schematic and work back to what gates it.
    5.

Golden rule: measure before you power

          
Before applying any power, measure each rail’s resistance/diode-mode reading to ground. Powering into a dead short destroys components. A near-zero reading on a main rail = hard short — don’t plug the charger in.

        

        

          
No power / short to ground

          
    
            
  1. Resistance first. Disconnect charger, battery and peripherals. Measure the main rail (e.g. 19 V input, then 3.3 V/5 V/1.8 V rails) to ground. Healthy diode-mode on a good rail is often ~0.3–0.5 V; below a few ohms on B+ to ground indicates a short.
    2. 
            
  2. Isolate the rail. Work out which rail is shorted — and whether it’s the board or the charger path. (Some laptops use an ADAPDET pin in the DC jack that reads 0 V until the adapter fully seats, so a bad jack can look like a fault.)
    3. 
            
  3. Voltage injection. Current-limited bench supply, ground on a solid board ground (screw/shield). Start low: ~0.5–1.5 V, limit ~1.5–2 A. If the supply instantly collapses to ~0.1 V, it’s in constant-current — that’s what a real short looks like, not a PSU fault.
    4. 
            
  4. Find the hot part. With current flowing, find what heats up: thermal camera, a smear of IPA (evaporates fastest over the hot part), or freeze spray. Usual culprits: shorted caps, MOSFETs, current-sense resistors.
    5. 
            
  5. Confirm on the schematic/boardview which component sits on that net before you remove anything — a short can sit anywhere on a big copper plane.
    6. 
          

          
Treat specific voltages/thresholds as rough guides — always check the board’s own schematic.

        

        

          
Won’t charge

          
    
            
  • Check the DC jack/USB-C port and the ADAPDET/CC lines first — bad ports are extremely common.
    • 
            
  • Verify the charging IC’s input rail and enable signals; check the current-sense resistors and the battery FETs.
    • 
            
  • USB-C: check the CC lines and the protection/e-marker; a blown TVS/short on VBUS stops charging.
    • 
          

        

        

          
No display / no backlight

          
    
            
  • Does it POST (fans, caps-lock, external monitor)? If external works, suspect panel/cable/backlight, not the GPU.
    • 
            
  • Backlight: check the backlight boost rail and enable, the fuse/filter near the connector, and the LED driver.
    • 
            
  • No output at all: check the GPU/SoC core rails and clocks.
    • 
          

        

        

          
Liquid damage

          
    
            
  • Don’t power on. Full teardown, inspect under the scope for corrosion/green crud, especially around connectors and power sections.
    • 
            
  • Ultrasonic clean (or IPA + brush), dry fully, then diagnose as a short/no-power board — corrosion creates leakage paths.
    • 
            
  • Replace visibly corroded components; corrosion wicks under shields and BGAs.
    • 
          

        

        

          
Boot loop / random shutdown

          
    
            
  • Thermal: reflow/re-paste, check fan. Shuts off within seconds of load → overheating or a failing rail.
    • 
            
  • Check for a rail that sags under load (scope/meter), marginal caps, or a failing VRM phase.
    • 
            
  • If it reaches Windows then BSODs → use Crash Analysis; if events show WHEA/hardware errors → Diagnose → Recent Problems.
    •

          Reading current signatures — quick reference
          

            

              
What you seeLikely meaning

              
0 mA, voltage presentOpen circuit / no power path — blown fuse, broken rail, bad connector, or the board isn't drawing (not turning on at all).

              
High mA instantly, voltage sags, stays flatDead short to ground — shorted cap, FET, PMIC or rail. Classic "won't power, gets hot". Find the hot component / use the short-finder.

              
Normal rise, then drops to ~0Protection trip / brown-out — draws, then a rail collapses and the PMIC shuts down (bad rail, short downstream, firmware halt).

              
Ramps up and stalls at a plateau, never completesStuck in boot — stops at a stage (e.g. no display, bootloop). Compare the plateau current to a known-good board.

              
Rhythmic pulsing of currentBoot-loop / reset loop — the board keeps restarting. Often a power-rail or storage fault.

              
Steady current climbing to a healthy peak then settlingNormal boot curve — inrush, OS/firmware load, then idle. Save this as your "good board" reference.

              
Negotiates 5 V only, won't go to 9/12/15/20 VUSB-C PD negotiation issue — CC lines, PD controller, or e-marker. Watch the voltage steps.

            

            
These are starting points, not certainties — always confirm with the board's rails and the fault-finding playbooks. Build your library: the more good/bad boards you record for a given model, the sharper "Match to library" gets.

New to BIOS chips? Start here

          
A motherboard’s firmware lives on a tiny SPI flash chip. Your XGecu T76 clips onto it and can read it (make a copy = a “dump”) or write to it (program a new image). This tab helps you understand a dump, fix it, find a clean one, and get it ready to flash — in plain English.

        


        

          
The safe workflow

          
    
            
  1. Read the chip with Xgpro on the T76 and save the dump as a .bin. Always keep that original untouched — work on copies.
    2. 
            
  2. Analyze a Dump here → hit Analyze. You get a verdict, confidence, and what-to-do-next.
    3. 
            
  3. If the board was tampered with or you want factory-fresh: Clean the ME region (it writes a new .cleaned.bin, original kept).
    4. 
            
  4. If the chip is blank or corrupt: Get Firmware → find a clean image for the exact board (Bad Caps / OEM).
    5. 
            
  5. Write the final image back with Xgpro, then verify the write.
    6. 
          

        


        

          
What to use, and when

          
    
            
  • Analyze — always first. Tells you what the image is, whether it’s healthy/complete, and your next move.
    • 
            
  • Clean ME region (me_cleaner) — neutralises the Intel Management Engine for a clean, factory-like state. Intel boards only; skip on AMD.
    • 
            
  • Full UEFI report / UEFITool — to look inside: firmware volumes, modules, settings. UEFITool also edits modules when you know what you’re changing.
    • 
            
  • Get Firmware — when you need a clean donor image because the chip is blank, corrupt, or wrong.
    • 
            
  • Xgpro — the only thing that actually reads/writes the physical chip. TM Toolset preps the file; Xgpro flashes it.
    • 
          

        


        

          
Golden rules

          
    
            
  • Keep the original dump. Name it board_original.bin and never overwrite it.
    • 
            
  • Match the exact board/model — a BIOS from a “similar” board can brick it or wipe its identity (serial/UUID/MAC).
    • 
            
  • After a chip swap you may need to restore the board’s original serial/UUID/MAC — note them down first.
    • 
            
  • If the Analyze step flags Boot Guard / FIT, flash a clean OEM image as-is — a modified/ME-cleaned image may refuse to boot.
    • 
            
  • Verify every write. A half-written chip is a dead board until re-flashed.
